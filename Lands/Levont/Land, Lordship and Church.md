# Land, Lordship and Church

How rule works below the council: who owns the land, how nobles work, who the bailiffs are, and what the churches and monasteries hold. This is the "middle layer" from [[Worldbuilding Principles]]. **Considerations only (started 21 Oct). Nothing here is canon yet.** Decisions go into [[Government of the Levont]] and [[Counties of the Levont]].

## The principle: nobody owns anything outright
Medieval power stayed interesting because land and rights were split up. One person owned the fields, another the hunting, a third the ore underneath, a fourth the court that judged the peasants. Every layer has someone above it who can't fully control it and someone below it who resents it. If the Levont works the same way, every dispute pulls in three or four parties.

## Who owns the land (suggestion)
| Land | Held by | Real-world model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| County lands | The count's house, in the union's name | English earls held land "of the crown" |
| Manors | Lesser nobles, in return for service | Anglo-Saxon thegns |
| Church lands | The churches and monasteries, tax-free | Everywhere in medieval Europe |
| Chartered towns | Themselves, answering only to the council | Free imperial cities of the Holy Roman Empire |
| Union lands | The council: the capital, way stations, railroad corridor, preserves | Royal forests under Norman forest law (the "Duke's Woods" idea, [[Preserves and Parks]]) |
| What's underground | The council, leased out | The Holy Roman Empire's mining right, which belonged to the ruler, not the landowner |
| Order lands | The [[Order of the Rising Sun]], under its own law | Holy Roman Empire "immediacy": answering only to the top |

**The underground row and the dwarves:** a count owns the mountain, the council owns the ore, and the Veingold hold the lease ([[Dwarves]]).

## Nobles
- **Nobility as a deal.** Borrowed from the Swedish *frälse*: you're noble because you're freed from tax in return for military service. A deal can be questioned, renegotiated or broken.
- **Two layers that resent each other:**
    - **Old blood:** titles from the [[Former Countries of the Levont|former countries]], older than the union.
    - **Union nobles:** made by the council since the founding, often for war service.

  This mirrors the Order's old guard and new guard.
- **Regents.** One idea: county lords are formally *regents*, ruling their county in the union's name. It means nothing day to day, but the council could use it to remove a lord, in theory.

## Bailiffs and the middle layer
This is where ordinary people meet power. Two chains of officials, with overlapping territory:
- **The lord's men:** stewards and bailiffs who collect rents and run manor courts.
- **The council's men:** reeves for union lands (the Anglo-Saxon *shire reeve* became the sheriff), plus travelling envoys who check up on the counts. Charlemagne's *missi dominici* went in pairs, one priest and one layman.

Bailiffs make great villains. See Engelbrekt below.

## Churches and monasteries
**The church as a power:**
- **Land and tithes.** Churches own land, often tax-free, and collect a tithe (a tenth of produce). Every acre given to a monastery is an acre the count can't tax.
- **Who appoints the high clergy?** The church itself, the counts, or the council? (Investiture, below.)
- **Church courts.** Can clergy be tried by a county court, or only by their own church? The Order already has its own justice. Do the churches too?
- **Sanctuary.** Can a fugitive claim protection inside a temple? Whose temples count?
- **Younger sons and daughters.** Noble families place spare children in the church, so abbots and high priests are often nobles' siblings. Church and nobility are tied by blood.

**Monasteries by god (suggestions).** Each house does a job the state can't, which is why rulers both need and envy them.

| God | House | What it does | Real-world model |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [[Lurian]] | Archive houses | Keep records, genealogies and **land deeds**. Whoever owns the land, Lurian's clergy wrote it down, and they could "lose" it | Monastic scriptoria and cartularies |
| [[The Sunturner]] | Hospitals | Healers and midwives, hospices for travellers. Ties to the Order | Knights Hospitaller |
| [[The Bud and the Sprout]] | Orchard and grange houses | Clear land, plant orchards, run model farms. Get rich doing it | Cistercians, who cleared land and grew rich on wool |
| [[The Martyr]] | Almshouses and granaries | Poor relief, famine stores. "The needs of the many" could justify the state seizing those stores | Almshouses, church granaries |
| [[Verdan Ironoath]] | Oath halls | Witness and record oaths of fealty, contracts and treaties | Oaths sworn on relics in churches |
| [[Tapio]] | Chapter houses (already in the vault) | Clerics, [[Green Wardens]] and druid circles | Military orders' commanderies |
| [[The Smiling Father]] | Burial grounds | Funerals, the returned (9.12) | Chantries, which prayed for the dead for payment |

**Military orders.** The [[Order of the Rising Sun]] already looks like the real military orders: the Templars (rich, lent money to kings) and the Teutonic Order (ruled its own state in Prussia).

## Real situations to borrow from
| Event | What happened | What it could be in the Levont |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Kalmar Union** (1397–1523) | Denmark, Norway and Sweden under one monarch, brokered by Queen Margaret. Swedes resented Danish dominance and foreign bailiffs. It ended after the Stockholm Bloodbath (1520) and Gustav Vasa's rising | The Levont is a union brokered by one old country. The closest real model for the broker's pride and the others' resentment |
| **Engelbrekt's rebellion** (1434) | Miners of Bergslagen rose against the union king's bailiffs | Miners of the salt steppe or the uplands rising against council reeves |
| **Magna Carta** (1215) | Barons forced King John to accept written limits on his power | The counties force a charter on the council or its chair |
| **The Investiture Controversy** (about 1076–1122) | Emperor and pope fought over who appoints bishops. Henry IV knelt in the snow at Canossa (1077) | Who appoints the high priests of the churches with council seats? |
| **Thomas Becket** (1170) | The king's men killed the archbishop after a fight over church courts | Church courts against county courts. A killed priest becomes a martyr who strengthens his church |
| **The fall of the Templars** (1307–1312) | Philip IV of France, deep in debt to the Templars, had them arrested, and the order was dissolved | The Order bills everyone for its services. What if the council owes it too much? |
| **Dissolution of the monasteries** (England 1536–41, Sweden at Västerås 1527) | The crown seized church lands and sold or granted them, creating a new landed class | A council short of money eyes church lands. Union nobles get rich on them |
| **The *reduktion*** (Sweden, 1680) | Charles XI took back crown lands earlier granted to nobles | The council wants back the land it granted during the war |
| **The *liberum veto*** (Poland-Lithuania, from 1652) | Any single deputy could dissolve the Sejm and void its decisions | What a county veto looks like when pushed too far |

## Open questions
- Nobility as tax freedom for service, with old blood and union nobles as rival layers?
- Should the council's institution seats become **estates** instead, like Sweden's Riksdag (nobles, clergy, burghers, peasants)?
- Are counts hereditary, or confirmed by the council? Is "regent" their title?
- Do church lands pay tax? Who appoints the high clergy?
- Do churches have their own courts, and is there sanctuary?
- Which gods run which kind of house? Are the neutral gods' houses as welcome as the Accords'? (9.2)
- Who keeps the land deeds?
