# Great Houses of the Levont

The human powers of the [[Levont]], built Westeros-style with a Germanic, Holy Roman Empire theme (decided 2 Nov). Each old country ([[Former Countries of the Levont]]) still has one great house at its head, with a seat, a sigil, words (a motto) and old grudges. The houses outlived the old borders: they hold land in several counties, so every council vote is a tangle of the old map and the new.

Source proposal: [Humans of the Levont: the great houses](https://claude.ai/artifact/JtaMYD4GgsA822bgS7B2AL). From Westeros we take only the structure (regions as remembered kingdoms, great houses, bannermen, old grudges, marriage as policy), never its houses, sigils, mottoes, places or plots.

## Decided (2 Nov)
- **The great-houses layer,** Westeros-style, with a Germanic theme.
- **The house names** below.
- **Holy Roman Empire titles:**
    - **Landgraf** (landgrave): the six county lords.
    - **Markgraf** (margrave): the lords of [[The March]]. Fits the [[Order of the Rising Sun]]'s Frankish ranks.
    - **Freiherr** (free lord, baron): small landholders who answer to a greater lord.
- **The Council of the Levont** (named 2 Nov): no special title for the voters. The county lords sit on the Council of the Levont and elect the council's chair. In practice the chair is always a Reikhart, and that is the fight ([[Government of the Levont]]).

## Waves of settlement (decided 2 Nov)
Humans came to the Levont in layers, each pressed under the next. **The wave names are placeholders** and may be renamed later.

| Wave | When | Who | What is left of them |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. The Eldfolk** | The Elden Era, over 600 years ago | The first humans, who built the city under the plains and its forge | A few hill and marsh villages with old rites, odd words in the common tongue, and the forge |
| **2. The Doorholders** | Before the Sundering | The people of the old border kingdom, in [[The March]] | The Chlodings (the march folk), House Childemar and the [[Order of the Rising Sun]] |
| **3. The Comers** | After the Sundering, about 550 to 450 years ago | The ancestors of the other four old countries. They came through the March gate and by sea, fleeing lands the Sundering ruined | Reikmark, the river country, the uplands and the breadbasket |
| **4. The latecomers** | The last century | Refugees from near [[Lorweir]], Frontier sellswords, Adarian merchants | Quarters in the cities and camps on the plains |

*What follows (suggestions, not canon):*
- **The Eldfolk are the old blood.** Small, poor and proud. Some great houses quietly claim Eldfolk descent ("we were here first"); others deny it ("peasant").
- **The Doorholders let the others in.** The Comers came through the March gate while the border kingdom held it: *"we held the door you walked through."* That grudge sits under the union, and under who holds the gate today.
- **The Comers took land** from the Eldfolk and the edges of the elven marshes. The elves remember it through rebirth; humans made it a founding legend. An Aelnor and a Felding tell the same story with opposite heroes.
- **The latecomers are the union's test.** If the Levont is a country, they become Levonters. If it is five old countries in a coat, they never will.
- **Three layers:** *everyone knows* "our people came over the mountains long ago". *Some know* there were humans here first. *Forgotten:* some Comers were fleeing a lord's war they fought on the losing side.

## Justice and the right of combat (decided 2 Nov)
- **Each old country keeps its own way** of settling disputes, used for different punishments and laws:
    - **Reikmark:** trial by document. Whoever holds the better paper wins.
    - **The river country:** fines. Every harm has a price, and families settle it.
    - **The uplands:** trial by oath, with oath-helpers who swear alongside; a false oath is punished harder than the crime.
    - **The breadbasket:** the village moot. Neighbours judge neighbours, and the bailiff watches.
    - **The march:** trial by combat.
- **Underneath all of them lies the oldest layer: trial by combat.** It is *meant* to be open for everything, to anyone.
- **But it can be refused without shame:**
    - **Nobles** may refuse a duel.
    - **High-ranking, skilled practitioners of certain martial arts** may judge a challenge unfair (a farmhand calling out a sword-master) and decline it.
    - In both cases **the challenge is left unanswered without anyone losing face, unless the challenger brings a champion** for their cause. A champion makes it a fair fight, and then refusing would cost face.
- *Suggestions (not canon):* hiring champions becomes a trade, and poor folk club together to pay one; famous champions gather their own folklore; the council's union law sits on top of all of this, written in Reikmark's words.

## The War of the Toll Chains (decided 2 Nov)
About 300 years ago, before the union. House Strommund chained the rivers to force tolls, and Gundberg's ore couldn't move. Gundberg answered by burning Kaufwerth. The two houses still don't intermarry.
- *Suggestions (not canon):* the rebuilt Kaufwerth keeps one blackened wall on purpose; a burnt chain-link is a Strommund heirloom, and a Gundberg mine-captain's insult is "chain-bearer"; the Veingold lent to both sides.

## The Reikharts' old enemies (decided 2 Nov)
The Reikharts chair the council, so their enemies are the first thing other lords reach for when they want leverage against them.
- **The Amalings, the old kings of Reikmark.** Before the union, the Reikharts overthrew them and took the old seat. The Amalings still hold land in Reikmark and claim an older right to lead. Any lord who wants to unseat a Reikhart chair props up an Amaling. (The name echoes the Amali, the real Gothic royal line.)
- **The dispossessed.** The houses whose land the Reikhart regent took back during the regency, 120 to 80 years ago. Not one house but a whole class of bitter nobles, spread across the counties.
- *Suggestions (not canon):* the Amalings and the dispossessed could find each other: an Amaling claim gives the dispossessed a banner, and the dispossessed give the Amalings numbers. The Reikharts keep an Amaling or two married into their own line, as hostages in all but name. The Amalings' sigil and words are still to pick.

*Note (2 Nov):* [[The Contest of the 41 Pillars]] was between a sun elf and a dwarf (Heleriam Aeloven and Bosurum Veingold), not the human houses, so it is not a human grievance.

## How bannermen break (decided 2 Nov)
From the Grainbound's middle-layer lesson ([[Grainbound Lessons]]): each house's followers are held by something different, and that decides how they break.
- **Debt: Strommund's boat houses.** Langkiel, Pechruder and Dreilicht owe Strommund money and shares. Loyal while trade pays. If the railroad drains the river trade and Strommund can't pay, they sell themselves to whoever can: a station-charter house, or the Veingold.
- **Blood: von Ackerfeld's branches.** Every branch is family, which feels unbreakable, but land is split between all children, so each generation fights over inheritance. They break at every succession, when the gathering at Aldbury is bought.
- **Oath: Gundberg's mine-captains.** Dwarven-style oaths that hold until the lord breaks his side first. Gundberg did, when it helped crush its own miners in the rising. The oaths are cracked, and the heir is trying to mend them.
- **Fear: Reikhart's bailiff families.** They serve because the Reikharts can ruin them on paper. They break the moment the Reikharts look weak, for example if an Amaling claim gains ground.
- **Memory: the Chlodings and the Childemars.** They love the old kings but follow the Order and the Odberts for pay. It never quite breaks and never quite acts, until someone gives them a reason.

## Marriage and fostering (decided 2 Nov)
- **Von Ackerfeld daughters** are the commonest alliance: there are many, and they bring grain rights, worth more than a coin dowry.
- **A Childemar bride** brings prestige and no money, so rich new houses pay dearly. The Odberts have been refused for three generations.
- **Strommund marriages are mergers.** Women own shares, so a bride brings boats; contracts run longer than charters.
- **Fostering:** the [[Order of the Rising Sun]] takes younger children of every house as squires. Honour, education and hostage-taking at once.

## Guest-right (decided 2 Nov, 12.4)
**The halved coin is a custom of old.** A host broke a coin and gave the guest half; to harm a guest was to break a sacred bond. **Now cutting coins is bad for the economy:** clipped and halved coins debase the money, and if people do it often it becomes a real problem.
- **A rich person's rite** (decided 2 Nov): everyday people could never cut coins; they need every coin they have. Breaking a coin was always a show of wealth by those who could afford to waste one.
- *Suggestions (not canon):* the council and the Veingold (who hold the debts and mint the trust) have banned or taxed it, so it survives as a proud, slightly illegal habit of great houses. People speak of a "gold guesting" and a "clay guesting", and a clay guesting binds just as hard.
- **Common folk use a clay token** (decided 2 Nov): a fired clay token, broken in two, each side keeping half. Not a stick: anyone can snap a stick at any time, while a token has to be made and kept for the purpose.
- *Suggestion:* the tokens are fired and marked by a potter or the parish, so a half can be matched and can't easily be faked. A household keeps a few on the shelf for guests. Old families still keep heirloom half-coins from famous guestings.
